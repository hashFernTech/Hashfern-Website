Where to start automating, if you've never done it before

Most businesses don't fail at automation because the technology is hard. They fail because they pick the wrong process to automate first, get a messy result, and decide the whole idea wasn't for them.

Here's the filter we use with new clients before we write a single workflow.

Look for the task that repeats, not the task that's painful

Painful tasks feel urgent, but they're often one-off problems: a bad client, a messy handover, a system that's about to be replaced anyway. Repeating tasks are different. If your team does something the same way more than a few times a week, it's a candidate, even if it only takes ten minutes each time. Ten minutes, five days a week, adds up to weeks a year.

Check that the steps are actually consistent

Automation works well on processes with a clear, repeatable shape: this input leads to this decision, which leads to this action. If every case is a special case, with different steps depending on who's doing it, that's a process that needs simplifying before it needs automating.

Start with one process, not your whole operation

The businesses that get the most out of automation don't try to automate everything in one go. They pick one process, get it working properly, and use what they learn to decide what's next. A few good starting points we see often in NZ SMEs:

  • Chasing quotes, invoices, or overdue payments
  • Booking and confirming appointments
  • Answering the same handful of customer questions, again and again
  • Moving information between two systems that don't talk to each other

Write down what "done well" looks like

Before any build starts, it helps to know what a good outcome actually looks like for that process, in plain terms. Not "faster," but something you can point to: fewer overdue invoices, appointments confirmed without a phone call, no more copy-pasting between two spreadsheets. That's what tells you, later, whether the automation actually worked.
